3.1 Research Design and Methodological Rationale



This research was conducted based on Design Science Research (DSR). DSR suits are used when constructing and testing artefacts to help solve a real-life problem. In this setting there is awareness training, but it is uniform, and it does not 

take into account

 the differences among students. The artifacts created were a set of exploratory learner profiles, requirements specification, adaptive training framework and a low fidelity prototype.



There were two inputs to the design process. The first was quantitative secondary data analysis of which data analysis was descriptive and exploratory. It addressed RQ2 and provided evidence for RQ3. The second was a 

small scale

 formative evaluation of the prototype using the System Usability Scale (SUS) and open comments which addressed RQ4. It was not identified as a mixed methods project. The components studied a variety of phenomena at various points and did not triangulate one phenomenon. They were only connected by the artefact: the profile shaped the 

requirements

 and the requirements shaped the prototype, which was evaluated.



There were three alternatives that were rejected. The platform is deployed and goes beyond MSc resources CITATION KarrenbauerC23 \p 2055 \l 1033 (Karrenbauer et al., 2023). A new primary survey would be a rehash of an existing public dataset and impose consent burdens. Sustained deployment and observed 

behaviour

 

is

 outside of the scope of a controlled 

behaviour

-change experiment. The project does not provide evidence of 

behaviour

 change 

as a result of

 

personalised

 training but rather provides evidence of design CITATION LeiteJ25 \p 22 \l 1033 (Leite et al., 2025).





3.2 Conceptual Framework and 

Operationalisation





Based on the knowledge–attitude–

behaviour

 perspective and adaptive learning logic, the conceptual framework (see Figure 3.1) was developed. It provides an understanding of the concepts that 

underlie

 measurement and design. Order of activities is described in the workflow in Section 3.4. These are not to be mistaken for the other two.



The knowledge was not applied in practice – no knowledge test was obtained. The closest items reflect self-reported awareness/confidence, like feeling informed about cybercrime, or knowing the dangers of pop-ups CITATION BrickleyJ26 \p 206 \l 1033 (Brickley et al., 2026). These are not known facts, but rather what is thought to be known. It also provides self-reported practices, security perceptions, concern, responsibility, context and exposure. There was no attempt to merge confidence and concern together and awareness and practice were retained as distinct constructs.



Indicators are used in the framework to provide information on the learner profiles to be explored. Profiles 

tell

 about training needs, requirements 

tell

 about module selection, sequence and level. Short module checks provide feedback and can edit the profile. A better score on a quiz in the prototype might be related to 

a feedback

 on the knowledge area but would not indicate better everyday security 

behaviours

 CITATION UchenduB21 \p 102410 \l 1033 (Uchendu et al., 2021). The framework regulated the 

variables selected

, the indicators that profiled and the writing of the illustrative recommendation rules, and the qualities assessed of the prototype.

3.6 Data Preparation and Quality Checks





Mis-spellings

 ('

Somtimes

' in Q20, 84 responses; ‘

Chemsitry

' in Q4) and stray spaces in labels were identified in the audit as well as 18 cells with only whitespace. Multiple-selection answers are recorded as a string of answers separated by commas; some answers include commas, so were matched to known strings of answers. Five pairs of exact repeated rows were found (workbook rows 135/136, 361/362, 471/476, 549/550 and 583/606). There are three pairs that are adjacent – this may be a sign of resubmission errors CITATION BrownP22 \p 1825 \l 1033 (Brown & Anderson, 2022). Repeated rows were marked (not removed

)

 and the results were compared to and without the second copy. No cleaning was applied at the audit stage meaning no retained record count was reported in this section.



Logic of skipping was not enforced. One hundred and 

forty four

 (144) out of 159 (31%) respondents who reported 

victimisation

 answered Q24 A, while 141 out of 456 (31%) who did not report 

victimisation

 answered Q24 A. All the students answered Q24 B. No 

one who

 chose the answer in Q15 ‘None of the above’ did not answer Q16 CITATION HallidayS22 \p 115 \l 1033 (Halliday et al., 2022). Only those who could be counted as eligible respondents were used and out-of-scope answers were reported separately by denominators. The responses of 'do not know' and 'not applicable' were kept separate from the 'no' response and were not represented by zero. P8 was the only one that was reverse scored. The pretest of inter-item correlations and Cronbach's alpha indicated that the Q14 items were not one scale, as risk-related items (P8, B2, B3) were correlated, but negatively with the protective practices. There was no internal consistency of the seven Q18 items, so no Q18 composite was constructed. If 

a reliability

 was reported and judged to be satisfactory, then a composite P3 to P7 was considered CITATION GreenD24 \p 8 \l 1033 (Green et al., 2024). Otherwise, the indicators would have stayed as separate indicators.





3.7 Data Analysis and Learner Profiling



The preparation of data and summaries was done using Python: pandas and NumPy libraries, association tests were performed with the SciPy library, data pre-processing was performed with the scikit-learn library, and clustering was carried out with the scikit-learn library. Frequencies and percentages for categorical items and medians with interquartile ranges for ordinal items with denominators stated were reported for descriptive analysis. Spearman correlation was used to check for monotonic relationships among ordered indicators. Categorical associations were analyzed using chi-square tests; the Fisher exact test or merged categories for 

small expected

 counts CITATION WagnerJ19 \p 1968 \l 1033 (Wagner et al., 2019). These analyses were descriptive, in nature, showing associative relationships. For a non-probability sample, p-values 

were considered to be

 descriptive measures only, and not population inferences.





The practice/

practice perception (P1 to P8 & B1 to B3) indicators were used for profiling. A later description of the profiles was based on 

the attitudes

, context and exposure. The ordinal items were re-coded into a 0–1 scale based on the rank position with the directions provided in Table 3.3. There was no guarantee that numeric codes would be evenly distributed, and so one possible implementation was K-means using the 

standardised

 codes, rather than the default CITATION PhelpsN25 \l 1033 (Phelps, 2025). This was conducted in parallel with hierarchical clustering using a Gower-type distance and if the indicators were found to be primarily categorical, K-modes 

was

 considered. To compare the solutions for each method, the elbow method (K-means), average silhouette scores, repeated runs, bootstrap agreement (calculated by the adjusted Rand index) and agreement between methods were used CITATION YahyaM25 \l 1033 (Yahya et al., 2025). Only solutions that contained 5% or more of the records in each 

cluster,

 were stable and had coherent summaries of the items were retained. If not, the structure was documented as weak and rule-based profiles were utilized instead. Labels were only selected upon a visual inspection.





3.8 Requirements and Prototype Development



Requirements were derived from literature and system review, analysis of the dataset, and learning 

programme

 guidance (including NIST SP 800-50 Revision 1). 

MoSCoW

 (Must, Should, Could, Won't) was applied to 

prioritise

 them CITATION RealeC26 \l 1033 (Reale et al., 2026). A traceability matrix with evidence, learner needs, requirements, design feature and evaluation check was established and a traceability chain was created that enabled the ability to follow the evidence up and down from the Must requirements. The initial set of proposals 

were

 modified as needed.



The artefact was a clickable wireframe and a set of rules documented, not working software. It included a brief introduction to the diagnostic, a summary of the profile, simple explanations of each recommendation, module pathways and feedback screens CITATION KhazenM23 \p 5 \l 1033 (Khazen et al., 2023). The decision of Rule Based Adaptation was made to allow the rules to be inspected and explained. The design was split into three layers, presentation, adaptation (scoring, profile assignment and rules) and data and content (profile definitions, a small catalogue of modules). The same rules and parameters were used to code and scale the diagnostic answers as used for the profiles, which allowed for comparison of a persona with a profile prior to assigning the persona to the closest profile CITATION 21 \p 51 \l 1033 (Лазурик et al., 2021). Recommendation rules were only suggested and not definite until the completed profile analysis confirmed the recommendation.





3.9 Evaluation Procedures



Five to eight adult volunteers (aged 18 or above as confirmed at consent) were used to evaluate the study. When respondents were asked about their own practices, no one provided a response because of 

fictionalisation

. The tasks involved were to finish the diagnostic as a persona, read the profile summary, and explain the reason for the recommendation of the first module, and complete a brief check and interpret the feedback CITATION FanT21 \p 14 \l 1033 (Fan et al., 2021). Scripted persona answers caused the screens to 

run

 and the path was traced along the rules that were recorded. Participants then filled in an online questionnaire comprising of the 10 SUS items, relevance and clarity items and open questions.



The total number of hits (Standard SUS scoring) was used. Odd items get the response minus 1, even items get 5 minus the response, the total is multiplied by 

2.5

 and 

the final result

 is a score out of 100 and not a percentage of usability. There were no criteria that were set in a 

favour

 way, the time of the data collection, a mean SUS of 68 or higher was required, at least 80% of persona tasks had to be completed without the support of the facilitators, and the median score for the added relevance and clarity items had to be 4 or higher out of 5 points. It was formative targets, NOT statistical tests. Open comments were thematically 

analysed

 and 

were connected with

 requirements. One or two cybersecurity-education experts performed a review with a brief checklist of criteria related to the clarity of the rules, their relevance and alignment with NIST guidance CITATION OffenbergerS19 \p 5 \l 1033 (Offenberger et al., 2019). The usability, perceived relevance, design quality and clarity of recommendations were assessed. No evaluation of learning gain or 

behaviour

 change.





3.10 Ethical Considerations and Data Management



Ethical consideration had not been considered or approved from the outset and no participant information was gathered prior to approval. Participants were provided with an information 

sheet

 and the informed consent was obtained and the participants were able to withdraw before data was 

anonymised

 CITATION TrowlerV19 \p 84 \l 1033 (Trowler et al., 2019). The responses were anonymous, with access to them restricted and on secure storage at the University, which was set to be erased at the end of the retention period defined by the University policy. The data set is a secondary and public data set and used under its 

licence

 with attribution. 

In order to

 no longer show individual responses, the youngest age band starts with 17 years, and the adult status isn't confirmed CITATION JancewiczB24 \p 682 \l 1033 (Jancewicz & Wrotek, 2024). The results are presented at group level with groups of less than five suppressed and the one for ‘Northern Borders’ not reported separately.





3.11 Methodological Limitations and Chapter Summary



All the indicators are self-reports and are subject to social-desirability and recall bias. The sample of the six universities of Bihar and IGNOU, which is non-probabilistic, is not generalizable, even if it is intended for students from the UK CITATION BubouG20 \p 16 \l 1033 (Bubou & Job, 2020). No validated questionnaire, many constructs are based on one item, and some response scales do not fit very well their statements. Clustering always comes back with groups and, therefore, profiles are 

exploratory

. 5-8 of the evaluators were not supportive of 

generalisation

 CITATION PuustinenM18 \p 191 \l 1033 (Puustinen et al., 2018). Reproducibility was assured by keeping the scripts, preparation logs, random seeds and library versions. This chapter has outlined a clear pathway for evidence to prototype. The data analysis 

reported

 in the next chapter.
