




Adaptive Cybersecurity Awareness Training for University Students: A Data-Driven Framework for Personalised Learning Design



CHAPTER 4: DATA ANALYSIS, FINDINGS AND DISCUSSION





4.1 Introduction



This chapter reports the analysis of the secondary dataset. It principally answers RQ2: which awareness and behavioural patterns the dataset shows, and how they can support learner profiling. It supports RQ3 by turning the findings into training requirements. It links the findings to the literature behind RQ1 and states what evidence for RQ4 is still missing. Section 4.2 audits and cleans the data. Section 4.3 describes the respondents. Sections 4.4 and 4.5 report practices, attitudes and associations. Section 4.6 identifies learner profiles, Section 4.7 derives requirements and Section 4.8 discusses the findings. Section 4.9 states the status of the framework, prototype and evaluation, and Section 4.10 summarises the chapter.

Coding followed Chapter 3. Frequency and agreement items were coded 1–5, digital skill 1–3, and experience and concern items 0–2, with the original direction retained. Reversed versions were created as separate variables only for the password and not-targeted items. Multiple-response questions were converted to option indicators, with a missing value only where the whole question was unanswered. Missingness was low: each Q14 item lacked one answer, and the demographic items lacked between 4 and 9. One respondent (Excel row 114) lacked all Q14 answers, so 614 respondents were clustered, without imputation. The cleaned workbook keeps the cleaned text beside every coded column.



Four kinds of problem were kept apart: missing answers, uncertain answers, structurally inapplicable answers and contradictory answers. ‘Do not know’ and ‘Not sure’ are substantive uncertainty categories, not zero scores. The five pairs of identical records may be repeat submissions or coincidence, so they were retained and a sensitivity analysis excluded the second copies (Section 4.6). The survey did not enforce its conditional questions. Branch-specific results therefore use eligible respondents only.





4.3 Respondent Characteristics



Table 4.2 summarises the sample. Men formed 60.6% of the 607 who stated a gender, and undergraduates formed 66.0%. Respondents spanned four age bands. The 17–25 band (37.1%) includes age 17, so adult status cannot be assumed. Most rated their digital skills as intermediate (58.1%). Use was intensive: 83.1% used the Internet throughout the day and 96.1% used a smartphone regularly. Mobile networks were the commonest connection (71.6%), and public Wi-Fi was uncommon (6.5%).



Fields of study were varied, and the largest were computer science (146) and education (126). Patna University supplied 45.7% of respondents. 72.5% rated their devices somewhat or very secure, and 11.4% were not sure. These are perceptions, not verified security. The sample is non-probability and geographically narrow, so the percentages describe these respondents, not Indian students generally.

4.4 Awareness, Attitudes and Reported Practices



Reported protective practices were frequent (Figure 4.1; Table 4.3). Often or Always was chosen by 86.6% for awareness of pop-up dangers, 85.0% for caution with links, 82.1% for privacy settings, 79.3% for checking websites and 77.9% for software updates. Risk-related items looked different. 48.5% reported Often or Always using personal information in passwords, and 47.2% held the belief that hackers would not target them. Only 28.8% felt safe on public Wi-Fi, and few used it.

Two further items need cautious reading. 59.0% reported changing important passwords often, and 52.4% reported reading terms and conditions. Frequent password change is not clearly protective, so neither item was treated as a safer-practice score. Respondents felt more informed than protected. 61.8% agreed that they felt informed about the cybercrime threat, but only 35.4% felt well protected. Privacy concern was strong: 69.4% were concerned that their personal information was not secure enough. Among the 40 public Wi-Fi users, 55.0% felt safe on it, compared with 26.9% of other respondents.



Tool use was uneven (Figure 4.2). Anti-virus software was reported by 68.1%, authentication (password or PIN) by 54.8%, firewalls by 36.4% and encryption by only 11.2%. The median respondent used 2 tool types (IQR 1–4); 28 used none. The authentication option does not establish multifactor use. Updating of security software was uncertain: 9.6% did not know, and 14.5% chose a self-contradictory label.

4.5 Associations and Group Comparisons



Five comparisons were declared in the analysis script before p-values were inspected (Table 4.5). Spearman correlation was used for ordered pairs and a chi-square test for skill and victimisation. Holm correction was applied across the five tests, with a 0.05 threshold. Self-rated skill was weakly and positively associated with perceived informedness (ρ = 0.19, n = 606) and website checking (ρ = 0.13). Perceived informedness was weakly associated with link caution (ρ = 0.15), as was phishing concern (ρ = 0.10, n = 577, excluding ‘Do not know’). All four remained below 0.05 after correction.



These associations are weak. Knowing one rating tells a designer little about the other, so self-rated skill is a poor guide to what a student needs to learn. The median informedness rating was 3 for beginners and 4 for intermediate and expert respondents. Victimisation did not differ significantly by skill category (χ²(2) = 4.63, p = 0.099, Cramér’s V = 0.09). The direction was unexpected: 20.4% of beginners, 26.1% of intermediate and 32.4% of expert respondents reported victimisation.



The tests rest on assumptions: independent observations, ordered categories treated as ranks, and adequate expected counts for the chi-square test. Non-probability sampling means the p-values cannot support conclusions about all students. They are exploratory guides, and the correlations show direction without showing cause. Excluding duplicate copies left every conclusion unchanged (Appendix C).

4.6 Learner Profiles



Eight features were clustered (Appendix B, Table B1): website checking, safer password practice (reversed), pop-up awareness, privacy attention, software updating, link caution, perceived informedness and perceived susceptibility (reversed non-target belief). Chapter 3 planned eleven indicators, including tool breadth, security-update status, perceived device security and public Wi-Fi safety. Those four were left out of the main solution because their categories are uncertain, conditional or perceptual, and perceived informedness was added. The complete Chapter 3 plan was run separately as sensitivity analysis S2b (544 complete cases). It agreed only moderately with the main solution (adjusted Rand index 0.55; 83.1% agreement). The main analysis therefore departs from Chapter 3, and this departure is a limitation of the profiling.



Features were standardised, and K-means (k = 2–6) used k-means++ starting points, 50 initialisations and seed 42 on the 614 complete respondents. Appendix C, Table C5 lists the stability procedures, software versions and label matching. Three feature pairs correlated at ρ ≥ 0.40, and five of the eight features are reported protective practices or awareness, so solutions mainly reflect overall reported caution.

Separation was weak. Silhouette values were 0.17–0.19, and no elbow was evident (Figure 4.3). The k = 2 solution had the highest silhouette (0.191), but it mainly separated lower from higher reported caution. The k = 3 solution (0.172) was retained because it was stable (subsample ARI 0.88), every group exceeded 25%, and it separated a design-relevant pattern. Solutions with k ≥ 4 were less stable. Hierarchical clustering with Gower distance gave near-singleton groups under average linkage (604 / 6 / 4) and a different partition under complete linkage (ARI 0.41). K-means is therefore an exploratory approximation, not evidence of natural clusters.



Table 4.7 and Figure 4.4 describe the profiles. Profile 1 (n = 222, 36.2%) reported consistently high practice, including safer passwords. Profile 2 (n = 230, 37.5%) reported high caution and informedness but frequent personal information in passwords (86.5% Often/Always) and low perceived susceptibility. Profile 3 (n = 162, 26.4%) reported moderate practice, lower informedness and more uncertainty. Silhouette values by profile were 0.17, 0.24 and 0.07, and 12.1% of respondents had negative values. The profiles are exploratory design aids with overlapping boundaries, and Profile 3 is the most diffuse. Outside the clustering features, Profile 3 had more beginners (37.7% against 16.9% in Profile 1) and more ‘Not sure’ device-security answers (21.6% against 7.2%). Identical Q14 answers occurred in 7.4% of Profile 2 and none of Profile 1, so response style may sharpen Profile 2.

The main interpretation was robust to duplicates and imputation but not to every analytical choice. 4.7 Translation into Adaptive Training Requirements



Table 4.8 traces each requirement from evidence to a proposed feature, using the presentation, adaptation and data/content layers of the framework. Requirements are proposed, not implemented, and are prioritised with MoSCoW. The modules are not equally supported. Password practice and phishing recognition rest on the strongest evidence, while updating and incident reporting rest on thinner or more uncertain evidence.



The survey is a design input, not a ready diagnostic. Likert self-reports and scenario performance are different measurements, so scenario scores cannot be treated as interchangeable with the survey features. A defensible approach has three steps. First, comparable self-report items could provisionally reuse the documented coding and standardisation parameters to suggest an initial profile. Second, scenario tasks could add evidence for choosing modules and difficulty. Third, matching profiles from scenario scores would need calibration and validation. Initial recommendations should remain provisional. Later reassessment would need new learner evidence, because this cross-sectional dataset cannot show updating.



The profiles suggest starting hypotheses, not fixed paths. For Profile 2, password practice and perceived exposure could come first, because respondents reported frequent risky password habits. For Profile 3, foundation-level phishing recognition and updating could come first, with fuller explanations, because respondents reported lower informedness and more uncertainty. Profile 1 could receive short refreshers. Reported caution does not show high skill, so difficulty would be set from diagnostic performance. This is a design hypothesis to test. Because the profiles overlap, each recommendation should carry its reasons and allow another choice. Demographic attributes would not be used. R6 rests on a small base (49 reporters; 32 destination answers) and R3 on one belief item, so both are marked Should. NIST SP 800-50 Revision 1 was written for federal agencies and their staff. Its planning and role-based principles could still guide programme structure for students, provided its organisational detail is not imported unchanged.

4.8 Discussion of Findings



Reported caution coexisted with risky password habits. Of 326 respondents reporting all four selected protective practices, 165 (50.6%) also reported Often/Always using personal information in passwords. Studies of undergraduates in other settings have likewise found reported attitudes stronger than reported behaviour, and awareness that did not reliably become protective behaviour. Other explanations also fit. Respondents may have read the password item loosely, answered ‘Always’ across items or given desirable answers, and 26 respondents gave one identical answer to all eleven items. The implication is that password practice needs rehearsal, not only information. The finding concerns self-reports, not observed passwords.



A separate finding concerns perceived susceptibility. 47.2% frequently held the not-targeted belief, and 159 of the 326 respondents reporting four protective practices (48.8%) held it too. The belief was strongest in Profile 2. It may reflect low perceived exposure or acquiescent answering, and one item is a thin basis. It supports an exposure explainer, not a firm claim about risk perception. Uncertainty was a third finding: between 31.2% and 45.0% chose ‘Do not know’ for experiences. This may reflect difficulty recognising incidents or unfamiliar terms, and the data cannot separate them.



Self-rated skill and informedness related only weakly to practice, and victimisation was not lower among expert respondents, possibly because experts detect more incidents. The HAIS-Q likewise measures knowledge, attitude and behaviour as separate dimensions, although it was developed for employees and this questionnaire is a different instrument. A diagnostic should therefore measure practice directly. Scenario items with known answers could also measure knowledge, which self-report cannot, but a quiz score would not show changed behaviour. Adaptive cybersecurity exercises elsewhere have used learner information to adjust difficulty, which supports the approach but not its effect in this setting.



The profiles were stable but overlapping (silhouette 0.17). Clustering returns groups whether or not clear structure exists, and the partition changed with the feature set (adjusted Rand index 0.55–0.64) and the method. The profiles therefore suit transparent, rule-based starting points, which supports explainable adaptation, and they are not discovered learner types.



Reporting results come from different respondents. Among victims who answered, 49 of 144 (34.0%) reported. Among non-victims, 431 of 456 (94.5%) said they would. The difference is not a measured intention–behaviour gap, because no respondent supplied both answers. The victim results show that many did not report, often because they fixed the problem themselves or did not know how to describe it. Destination results rest on 32 answers.



Several limitations shape interpretation. All indicators are self-reported, so reported caution is not demonstrated ability. The dataset has no objective knowledge test, so ‘knowledge gaps’ means reported limitations and uncertainty. The sample is non-probability, drawn from India, dominated by Patna University (45.7%) and includes one unverifiable university entry. Missing, uncertain and repeated records changed little, but unenforced skip logic and ambiguous labels, such as ‘No, I think it is automatically updated’, weaken some items. K-means on ordinal items assumes equal spacing. The data are cross-sectional and there is no pre/post evidence, so associations are not causal and the study cannot show that training works, a limitation common in evaluations of cybersecurity training.





4.9 Framework, Prototype and Evaluation Status



For Objective 3, three outputs were completed: the conceptual framework in Chapters 2 and 3, eight evidence-derived requirements, and illustrative adaptation rules arranged in presentation, adaptation and data/content layers (Table 4.8). No wireframes, user journeys, diagnostic instrument or recommendation screens were supplied, so no prototype implementation is described. No walkthrough records, System Usability Scale responses, open comments or expert feedback were supplied either. The adaptation logic exists only as the rules in Table 4.8, and no diagnostic items have been written or tested. Usability, relevance and design quality have therefore not been assessed, and RQ4 has not been empirically answered. The sparse ‘Other’ survey responses concern cybercrime, not the prototype, and were not treated as feedback. Objective 1 was addressed in Chapter 2 and Objective 2 in this chapter. Objective 3 is partly achieved, and completing it requires the prototype and its evaluation.





4.10 Chapter Summary



The analysis showed frequent reported protective practices alongside frequent risky password habits, a recurring not-targeted belief and substantial uncertainty about incidents. Weak but stable exploratory analysis identified three overlapping profiles, best used as provisional design aids. The findings supported eight proposed requirements, led by password practice and phishing recognition. RQ2 is answered for the dataset, and RQ3 is supported by the traceability table. RQ1 is addressed through the literature, while RQ4 remains outstanding until the prototype is built and evaluated. The next chapter draws conclusions and recommendations.
