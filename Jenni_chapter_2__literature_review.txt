






CHAPTER 2: LITERATURE REVIEW





2.1 Introduction



The literature survey is conducted in this chapter to provide background to the analysis, profiling and design efforts in this dissertation. It brings together four areas: cybersecurity awareness among university students, adaptive and 

personalised

 learning, current cybersecurity trainings systems and data-driven learner profiling. The areas are examined as part of Objectives 1 to 3 and Research Questions 1 to 3 and in Chapter 3 as part of the evaluation process. The chapter starts by exploring the 

conceptualisation

 and measurement of awareness, knowledge, attitudes and 

behaviour

 with students, and then moves on to adaptive learning techniques, current training provision, and profiling. The chapter concludes with an integrated conceptual framework, and the resulting research gap.





2.2 Adaptive Learning and 

Personalised

 Learning Systems





Personalisation

 and adaptation are two different concepts. 

Personalised

 learning is where a learner is given an initial pathway based on information which is collected at one point in time, for example, a diagnostic test given at the start of training. Adaptive learning, however, adapts the content, sequence and difficulty as more evidence about the learner is collected throughout the learning process CITATION DziubanC18 \p 9 \l 1033 (Dziuban et al., 2018). An adaptive system is thus dependent upon a continuous interplay between what the system 

has of the learner

 and what it provides for the learner next.



The architectures of most adaptive systems are similar. A learner model keeps information for a learner, including diagnostic information and/or more recent performance. Content model: Describes what is available, the organization of content by topics and difficulty. Adaptation rules link the two, determining what evidence the current model of the learner should infer for the next piece of content that the learner should view. The learners' model is updated based on feedback from subsequent activity, thus closing the loop and letting the path take on a trajectory that evolves over time, not just determined at the beginning CITATION WangT25 \p 5 \l 1033 (Wang et al., 2025).



There are two general methods of building adaptation rules. The specific conditions are explicitly set by transparent, rule-based logic, such as 'if you show weak 

behaviour

 but strong knowledge, then recommend 

practising

 on passwords. Machine-learning methods, in turn, are used to learn rules statistically from data; these may be more complex but generally require more 

large labelled

 data 

sets, and

 are more difficult for the learner or designer to examine  CITATION SarkerI22 \p 1491 \l 1033 (Sarker, 2022). In the field of education, this distinction is particularly relevant, as opaque algorithms may demoralize learners and institutions and can be considered as a feature of an AI design rather than an optional one, in terms of its ability to explain itself, according to Khosravi et al. (2022).



It was more suitable for this dissertation to use a transparent, rule-based approach rather than a machine-learning approach. Size of the available dataset is small and not collected specifically for the purposes of model training, explanations must be provided so that recommendations can be explained to students and reviewers, and a low fidelity prototype needs to be accessible within the limited timeframe of an MSc project CITATION ChazetteL20 \p 510 \l 1033 (Chazette & Schneider, 2020). This was a pragmatic decision due to the constraints of the project, and not a statement that 

rule based

 adaptation is better overall.





2.3 Cybersecurity Awareness Among University Students



There are differences between awareness, knowledge, attitudes and 

behaviour

. Knowledge relates to what people are able to say correctly about a risk or safeguard; attitude relates to people's evaluation of a risk or safeguard; 

behaviour

 refers to what people do; and awareness is often considered as a fourth construct, but in practice is the combination of knowledge, attitude and 

behaviour

. Survey research generally does not record self-reported 

behaviour

 – so, a high 

behaviour

 score does not mean that the respondent did what they reported they did CITATION StikvoortB17 \p 1612 \l 1033 (Stikvoort et al., 2017). Self-confidence, of course, does not equal knowledge that has been demonstrated and tested and the two are not interchangeable in the evaluation of results.



The Knowledge-Attitude-

Behaviour

 paradigm has played a significant role in the structuring of this area, for example, the Human Aspects of Information Security Questionnaire (HAIS-Q). Initially developed and tested with employees in 

organisational

 environments, HAIS-Q covers aspects such as password usage, email usage, social media usage, mobile device use and incident reporting. It is not a direct replication, but rather an extension of the instrument, when used with students CITATION McCormacA17 \p 3 \l 1033 (McCormac et al., 2017). For instance, Xu et al. (2024) modified HAIS-Q for Chinese college students and found a correlation between the students' scores on Attitude and 

Behaviour

 in which Attitude was higher, while 

Behaviour

 was lower, as the students completed their studies. Likewise, the results in South Africa by Goliath, 

Tsibolane

 and Snyman (2025) indicated that postgraduates had better awareness than the undergraduate students, this indicating that the academic stage rather than discipline affects awareness.



The results of these studies cannot be directly compared due to varying countries, sample composition, adaptation of HAIS-Q and analysis methods. Generalizations regarding the attitude-

behaviour

 patterns of Chinese undergraduates should not be made without supporting evidence for other groups, such as South African and Indian student groups CITATION IslamiR23 \p 9 \l 1033 (Islami et al., 2023). However, what is consistent 

at

 all settings is the awareness-

behaviour

 gap, that is, that high and even high knowledge and attitude scores are not consistently correlated with safer reported 

behaviour

. In a similar study, Ahamed et al. (2024) discovered that students' knowledge and practices around the password were related to their attitude, which was in turn correlated with overall awareness, in this case separating the constructs from the overall outcome. Setiawan and Rizal (2024) also note that 

awareness levels

 experienced after significant disruption (such as the Covid-19 pandemic) are context and time-sensitive, and not just the individual.



From a practical point of view, the use of training that focuses only on knowledge is unlikely to be sufficient to close the gap between knowledge and 

behaviour

; training that requires the student to 

practise

 specific activities related to the domain of the phishing attack, including phishing recognition, password practices, privacy settings, software updates, device protection and incident reporting, is more likely to achieve a desired 

behaviour

 change than further factual content.





2.4 Existing Cybersecurity Awareness Training Systems



There are a variety of types of university and 

organisational

 cybersecurity training including: awareness raising courses, self-paced e-learning courses, simulated phishing training, hands-on technical training and gamified training. 

Prümmer

, van Steen and van den Berg (2024) concluded that, after 

analysing

 142 studies on workplace cybersecurity training, most interventions showed some positive outcomes, regardless of the training content or the training method adopted (most often a game-based method), and that most evaluation studies were based on small samples and pre-test and post-test designs CITATION PrmmerJ23 \l 1033 (Prümmer et al., 2023). This suggests real potential in 

training as a whole, and

 it also suggests that there is less evidence to support claims of effectiveness in the field than might appear from the fact that they are so common CITATION ShewchukS23 \p 1 \l 1033 (Shewchuk et al., 2023).



One pathway taken with this training has been seen 

in

 commercial platforms. 

Hoxhunt

 creates a profile for each user based on information provided during onboarding and subsequent simulated-phishing work and then makes the simulated phishing and 

microtraining

 progressively more difficult. KnowBe4's Risk Score incorporates a percentage from both the training results and 

simulated-phishing

 to target additional training to individuals or groups with the highest risk. Microsoft Attack Simulation Training is a simulated attack campaign that is run in Microsoft 365 and automatically assigns follow-up training to users who are tricked by a simulated attack CITATION HydariM26 \l 1033 (Hydari et al., 2026). These are descriptions of a product published by the vendor and not independently peer-reviewed assessments, so statements about the effectiveness of these products should be interpreted as the vendors' own and not as proven effectiveness. summarises

 them.

The constraints for these systems apply to this dissertation. They are designed for employees within an 

organisation

 where accounts are provided centrally, not students; they do not provide open access for inspection of their adaptation logic; they focus mainly on phishing, not the wider range of domains covered by instruments like HAIS-Q; and they do not raise consent issues like running simulated attacks on students, which they would do in an employer-employee context CITATION DistlerV21 \p 26 \l 1033 (Distler et al., 2021). Nevertheless, despite this mismatch, NIST SP 800-50 Revision 1 is a relevant source of learning-

programme

 guidance: it applies to federal agencies which develop 

organisation

-wide learning 

programmes

 for their own employees, but its principles of lifecycle planning, role-appropriate content and continuous measurement are quite generic and readily adapted to a university training framework, provided they are not introduced uncritically. University cybersecurity courses that integrate virtual laboratories, gamification and simulation have been reviewed and these are the tools that enable hands-on and motivating learning, primarily in computing courses, but in some cases in the context of awareness provision throughout the university CITATION ArduinP26 \l 1033 (Arduin & Costé, 2026).





2.5 Data-Driven Learner Profiling



In differentiated training, there 

has to

 be some method of categorizing students with similar needs. There are two general strategies. The main advantage of rule-based profiling is that it assigns a learner to a category in a transparent and easy to justify manner based on pre-established thresholds on construct 

scores, but

 requires the thresholds to be set sensibly ahead of time CITATION BenabbesK22 \p 65 \l 1033 (Benabbes et al., 2022). Clustering is alternative to grouping respondents according to their answers, in the sense that patterns are not imposed on the 

data, but

 emerge from the data.



However, clustering always produces clusters, 

whether or not

 there is any structure in the data, of course: if the data are random, then the algorithm will generate random clusters. It cannot be assumed that the quality and stability of a clustering solution is good, but must be checked, for instance by comparing the solution for different numbers of clusters and the extent to which each cluster is separated and consistent, as well as by checking that similar clusters reoccur between multiple runs or resampling CITATION MontiS03 \p 114 \l 1033 (Monti et al., 2003). There are also issues with how the variables are constructed: scores that are built by combining several of the survey items must have a defensible and consistent scoring method, and categorical versus continuous variables might call for clustering approaches that are appropriate to that type of data as opposed to those that assume the numerical distance CITATION HorneE20 \p 14 \l 1033 (Horne et al., 2020).



Any profile that is created is affected by the variables selected. Profiles are indicators of learning need, based on the specific items used for the analysis (rather than fixed descriptions of ability or fixed types of 

student

) and a learner's profile may vary 

if

 different items, or a different clustering method, were used. This dissertation also did not include demographic characteristics (e.g., gender or discipline) in its clustering nor in subsequent recommendations. The aim of this was to limit one obvious source of demographic bias to the system, but it did not make it clear that resulting profiles and recommendations were fair, as bias may also be introduced indirectly via factors that are correlated with demographic characteristics. Table 3 and Chapter 3 describe the detailed procedure, the construction of variables and the outcomes of the clustering in this chapter.





2.6 Integrated Conceptual Framework



Through the literature reviewed above it is possible to combine the two ideas in one single conceptual framework that will guide the rest of this dissertation (Figure 2.1). An exploratory learner profile is fed by four awareness related indicators, based on the Knowledge-Attitude-

Behaviour

 approach in which the approach was extended by adding an exposure indicator, reflecting the platform and risk context of students CITATION PopoolaO24 \p 821 \l 1033 (Popoola et al., 2024). This evidence, combined with the adaptive learning literature reviewed in Section 2.2, is used to inform each profile 

with

 training requirements. 

Personalised

 module recommendations and ordering of content are based on requirements and represent a learning path. Short checks embedded in that pathway give feedback that can change the learner profile so that the pathway can change over time, not just once it has been made.

2.7 Research Gap and Positioning of the Study



The problem identified in the literature reviewed above is summed up in three points. Learner awareness research, including HAIS-Q and adaptations, does not only inform the measurement of learners' awareness, but also rarely informs the design of training CITATION Tond18 \p 3 \l 1033 (Ton et al., 2018). Research on adaptive learning is more advanced in 

the general

 education than in 

cybersecurity, and

 is used only infrequently for university-wide awareness training. Current 

personalised

 training platforms are more well developed for employees than for students, and their adaptation logic is not open to academic investigation, but proprietary CITATION edaP21 \p 2 \l 1033 (Šeda et al., 2021).



This is not a statement that there is no research about, but instead a reflection of the lack of supporting specific combination of secondary-data analysis, learner profiling and training design for university students. Individual components (such as HAIS-Q validation studies or adaptive cybersecurity exercises) are generally reasonably well established on their own CITATION IfenthalerD20 \p 2141 \l 1033 (Ifenthaler et al., 2020). What is relatively undeveloped is a single, identifiable process that takes students from secondary evidence to exploratory profiling to documented design requirements to evaluated prototype, done in such an open manner that they can be studied and repeated by other researchers CITATION WilsonN17 \p 504 \l 1033 (Wilson et al., 2017).



This gap directly relates to the aims and questions of the dissertation. The lack of a feature review specifically aimed at 

University

 students instead of employees is addressed by Objective 1 and RQ1. The lack of profiling work done on this specific data set is addressed by Objective 2 and RQ2. Objective 3, RQ3 and RQ4 address the lack of documentation from profiles to requirements to a tested low fidelity design. The following aspects were considered in the evaluation of usability, relevance and design quality within the scope of this project: Does this artefact function as expected? Does it meet the expectations of the intended users and domain experts? Does the 

personalised

 training lead to a change in 

behaviour

, which becomes apparent over time, in the real world? This question is left open for future 

research..
